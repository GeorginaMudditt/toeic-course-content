Writing Clearly

Professional writing does not have to be overly complicated. In fact, the aim is to communicate complex information in a way that is easy to follow. Clear writing helps your reader understand and act quickly, and it often sounds more confident than long, dense sentences.

In this lesson you will spot unclear writing, practise rewriting it, and complete three timed challenges that you can submit for teacher marking.

Warm-up: Spot the problems

Consider this example:

“Due to the fact that the implementation of the new authentication system has resulted in a number of issues relating to user access, it has been deemed necessary to undertake an investigation into the potential causes of these issues in order to ascertain whether or not any further action may be required.”

1 sentence · 52 words · level B2+

Can you see any issues with the example?

Discuss with your teacher before revealing the answer.

Look for: unnecessary words, vague language, repetition, overly complex wording, one very long sentence, and a lack of clarity about what actually happened or what the call to action is.

Can you rewrite the example, making it clearer?

“The new authentication system is preventing some users from logging in. We are investigating the cause and will decide what action is needed.”

2 sentences · 23 words · level B2

Tips for Writing Clearly

Avoid vague language

For example:

“There seems to be a fairly significant issue with some of the figures.”

Discuss: What's wrong with it? How could it be improved?

“The Q2 figures contain a €12,450 discrepancy.”

Avoid wordiness

For example:

“In light of the recent changes that have been made to the procedures relating to the submission and subsequent approval of requests for annual leave, all members of staff are kindly requested to familiarise themselves with the updated process, which will come into effect from 1 September, and to ensure that any future requests for holiday leave are submitted via the new online system in accordance with the revised procedure.”

Discuss: What's wrong with it? How could it be improved?

“From 1 September, please book all holidays through the new online system. See the updated procedure before making your next request.”

Discuss: Why do some people write like this? What do you think about the misconception that “longer = more professional”?

Avoid overuse of passive structures

Discuss: What is the passive structure? When is it useful?

Sometimes we need to use the passive, but it can become heavy if overused. For example:

“The report was prepared by our team and was reviewed by the manager before being submitted.”

can be rewritten much more clearly as:

“Our team prepared the report, then the manager reviewed it before submission.”
