Reading: My Internship Experience

Warm-up

Discuss these questions.

  1. Have you ever done an internship or work placement? What was it like?
  2. What do you think makes an internship successful — for the intern and for the company?
  3. What challenges might someone face during their first experience of professional life?
  4. Would you rather have a highly structured internship, or one that gives you more independence? Why?
  5. When you describe a work experience, how do you balance positive and negative points without sounding either naive or ungrateful?

Antonio Pinelli — My Internship Experience

Last summer, I completed a three-month internship at a medium-sized technology consultancy in Bristol. I had hoped that the placement would give me a clearer idea of what it was like to work in a professional environment, rather than simply allowing me to observe other people doing their jobs. Overall, the experience was very good1, although it was not without its challenges. I was particularly impressed by the variety of tasks I was given and by how quickly I was treated as a member of the team. That said, there were also a few aspects of the placement that I found bad2, particularly towards the busiest stages of the project.

One of the strongest aspects of the internship was the work itself. Rather than being given a series of routine administrative tasks, I was involved in several good3 projects that required me to think independently and apply what I had learned at university. My responsibilities gradually became more demanding, which I found both motivating and rewarding. The workload was generally good4: there was enough work to keep me occupied without leaving me feeling overwhelmed. Furthermore, my manager made a point of explaining why particular tasks mattered, which gave me a much clearer understanding of how individual pieces of work contributed to the company’s wider objectives. As a result, even when I made mistakes, I felt that I was learning from them rather than simply being criticised.

Equally importantly, the people I worked with made the experience considerably more enjoyable. My supervisor was approachable and supportive, and I never felt that asking questions would be interpreted as a sign of incompetence. The wider team was also good5 at communicating, particularly when several people were working on the same project. In addition, the office itself was good6: there were comfortable workstations, several quiet areas for meetings and a well-equipped kitchen. I was also pleasantly surprised by the company’s flexibility. Although interns were expected to be in the office most of the time, I was occasionally allowed to work remotely when I needed to concentrate on a particularly demanding task. This relatively good7 balance between independence and supervision helped me become much more confident in managing my own time.

Nevertheless, the experience was not entirely good8. As the end of my placement approached, the workload increased considerably because the team was working towards an important deadline. At times, the amount of work became bad9 enough that I struggled to complete everything within normal working hours. Although I understood that this was partly due to the temporary pressure of the project, I occasionally felt that expectations were unrealistic for an intern who was still learning the company’s systems. On a few occasions, I was also given urgent tasks without much explanation, meaning that I had to spend time working out what was expected before I could actually begin. In hindsight, better planning would have made this period considerably less stressful.

Another aspect that could have been improved was communication between different members of the organisation. While my immediate team was good10 at keeping me informed, information from other departments was sometimes less clear. There were also a few bad11 experiences with the office facilities. The meeting rooms were frequently fully booked, and the internet connection was occasionally unreliable when several people were working remotely. These issues were relatively minor, but they became more frustrating when deadlines were approaching. Similarly, some of the internal procedures were unnecessarily complicated, and I initially found it bad12 that I had to seek approval from several different people before being able to carry out fairly straightforward tasks. Although none of these problems seriously affected my internship, they did highlight some areas where the company could become more efficient.

On balance, however, I would describe the internship as a good13 experience and would certainly recommend the company to other students looking for their first taste of professional life. The opportunity to work on meaningful projects, receive support from experienced colleagues and gradually take on more responsibility was particularly valuable. Despite the bad14 periods of pressure and the occasional organisational problems, I came away with a much better understanding of both the realities and expectations of the workplace. More importantly, the placement helped me identify the skills I still need to develop before entering full-time employment. If I were offered the opportunity to do the internship again, I would accept without hesitation.

Discourse markers

Discourse markers are words and phrases that show how ideas are connected. They do not usually carry the main meaning of a sentence; instead, they tell the reader what the writer is doing with the next idea — adding a point, contrasting it, showing a result, looking back, and so on.

They help you in two ways. When you read, they let you predict the direction of a paragraph before you process every word, which is essential for efficient reading. When you write, they make your argument easier to follow and stop a text from sounding like a list of disconnected opinions.

Antonio’s article is full of them. For example, That said warns you that a reservation is coming; Nevertheless introduces the first real criticism; On balance signals that he is about to weigh everything up.
